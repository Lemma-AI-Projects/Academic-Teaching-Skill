# Speaker Notes — Version A (Standard)

## Slide 1: Title
Welcome to our introduction to Partial Differential Equations. Today we'll cover the basics of PDEs, including what they are, why they matter, and how we solve them.

## Slide 2: Outline
Here's what we'll cover today. We'll start with the definition, move to why PDEs are important, look at the three classical types, discuss classification, boundary conditions, and solution methods.

## Slide 3: Definition
A partial differential equation involves an unknown function u of several variables. The partial derivatives tell us how u changes with respect to each variable independently. The general form involves the function, its first and second partial derivatives.

## Slide 4: Why PDEs Matter
PDEs are everywhere. Weather prediction, aircraft design, heat transfer, brain modeling, financial markets, population dynamics — all of these use PDEs. They're the mathematical language for continuous change in space and time.

## Slide 5: Three Classical PDEs
Here are the three most important PDEs. The heat equation describes diffusion. The wave equation describes propagation. The Laplace equation describes equilibrium. Each has a different character.

## Slide 6: Classification
We classify PDEs using the discriminant B squared minus AC. If it's negative, the equation is elliptic. If zero, parabolic. If positive, hyperbolic. This classification determines the solution method.

## Slide 7: Boundary Conditions
You can't solve a PDE without boundary conditions. Initial conditions give you the starting state. Boundary conditions tell you what happens at the edges. There are three types: Dirichlet, Neumann, and Robin.

## Slide 8: Solution Methods
The main analytical method is separation of variables. We assume the solution is a product of functions, which splits the PDE into ODEs. For practical problems, we use numerical methods: finite differences, finite elements, or finite volumes.

## Slide 9: Summary
Let's review. PDEs involve functions of multiple variables. Three classical types exist. Classification matters. Boundary conditions are required. We solve them using separation of variables or numerical methods.
