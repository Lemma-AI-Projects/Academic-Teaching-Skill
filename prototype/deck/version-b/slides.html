The Language of Change

Partial Differential Equations

How we describe anything that changes in space AND time

Speaker Notes: Don't start with equations. Start with the world. "Every time you check the weather, you're looking at the output of a PDE solver. Every time an engineer designs an aircraft, they solve PDEs. Every time a doctor models how a drug spreads through tissue, they use PDEs. Today we'll see what these equations are and why they work."
1

From ODE to PDE

ODE — What You Know

One variable changes

dy/dt = f(t, y)

A ball falling in a straight line. One dimension. One direction.

The derivative tells you the rate of change in ONE direction.

PDE — What's New

Multiple variables change

∂u/∂t = α ∂²u/∂x²

Temperature on a metal rod. Two dimensions: position AND time.

The partial derivative tracks change while holding other variables fixed.

Speaker Notes: "You already know derivatives. A partial derivative is just a derivative where you hold some variables fixed. The jump from ODE to PDE is not about new math — it's about new geometry. We're no longer moving along a line. We're moving through space."

What a PDE Looks Like

A PDE is an equation involving an unknown function of several variables and its partial derivatives.
F(x, y, u, ∂u/∂x, ∂u/∂y, ∂²u/∂x², ∂²u/∂x∂y, ...) = 0
u
unknown function
what we're solving for
x, y
independent variables
space, time, etc.
∂
partial derivatives
how u changes
Speaker Notes: Walk through each symbol. Don't rush. "The u is what we're trying to find — it might be temperature, pressure, displacement. The x's tell us WHERE. The partial derivatives tell us HOW it changes. The equation itself is the RULE that governs the change."

Three Equations, Three Kinds of Change

Heat Equation

∂u/∂t = α∇²u
Parabolic
Temperature spreads out. Sharp gradients smooth over time. Diffusion.

Wave Equation

∂²u/∂t² = c²∇²u
Hyperbolic
Disturbances travel at speed c. Shape preserved. Propagation.

Laplace Equation

∇²u = 0
Elliptic
No time. Every point equals its neighbors' average. Equilibrium.
Speaker Notes: Don't derive. Just introduce. "These three equations are the atoms of PDE theory. Notice the second time derivative in the wave equation — that means acceleration, which means the disturbance PERSISTS. In the heat equation, there's only a first time derivative — that means the disturbance FADES."

The Heat Equation: Watching Diffusion Happen

A hot spot on a metal rod spreads out and smooths over time.
t = 0 (initial)
t = 1 (spreading)
t = 5 (smoothing)
t = ∞ (flat)
Speaker Notes: "Imagine you touch a cold metal rod with a hot finger. The heat spreads. That's the heat equation. Notice how the sharp spike becomes smooth — that's diffusion. The key insight: the rate of smoothing is proportional to the CURVATURE. Where the profile is sharpest, diffusion is fastest."
5

The Wave Equation: Watching Propagation Happen

A pulse on a string travels without losing its shape.
t = 0 (plucked)
t = 1 (traveling)
t = 2 (traveling)
t = 3 (near edge)
Speaker Notes: "Now imagine plucking a guitar string. The pulse travels. It doesn't smooth out like heat — it PRESERVES its shape. That's the fundamental difference between parabolic and hyperbolic. The second time derivative means acceleration — the pulse has momentum. It bounces off boundaries."

Why Classification Is Not Just Naming

The classification tells you the PHYSICS, not just the math.
Elliptic
No time dependence
Information: instant equilibrium
Steady state
Parabolic
First time derivative
Information: infinite speed, decaying
Diffusion
Hyperbolic
Second time derivative
Information: finite speed, preserved
Wave propagation
The discriminant B² - AC determines which column you're in → which physics applies → which solution methods work.
Speaker Notes: "This is not just naming. If I tell you the equation is hyperbolic, you already know: waves travel at finite speed, you need initial AND boundary conditions, and you should use methods that respect propagation. If it's parabolic, you know diffusion happens, information spreads infinitely fast (but decays), and you only need initial conditions. The classification is a shortcut to understanding the physics."

Why a PDE Alone Is Not Enough

The PDE is the rule. Boundary conditions are the specific situation. Same rule, different situation, different answer.
BCs: hot top, cold rest
BCs: hot bottom, cold rest
BCs: hot top+bottom
Same equation (∇²u = 0). Three different boundary conditions. Three completely different temperature distributions.
Speaker Notes: "The PDE is the rule — Laplace's equation says 'at every point, the value equals the average of its neighbors.' But that rule alone doesn't tell you the temperature distribution. The boundary conditions — what's happening at the edges — determine the specific solution. This is why you can't just 'solve a PDE' without specifying the boundary conditions."

The Key Trick: Separation of Variables

One hard PDE becomes two easy ODEs.
Hard PDE
∂u/∂t = α ∂²u/∂x²
→
Assume
u(x,t) = X(x)·T(t)
→
Split into
Two ODEs
→
Solve each
X(x) and T(t)
→
Combine
u = X·T
u(x,t) = Σ Bₙ sin(nπx/L) e-α(nπ/L)²t
Each term is a product: spatial part × temporal part
Speaker Notes: "This is the key trick. We assume the solution is a product of a function of x only and a function of t only. When we substitute into the PDE, the x-parts and t-parts separate — we get two ODEs instead of one PDE. ODEs we know how to solve. Then we combine the solutions. The Fourier coefficients Bₙ come from matching the initial condition."

Speaker Notes: "We've seen the full arc. PDEs describe change in space and time. The three classical types — elliptic, parabolic, hyperbolic — correspond to three kinds of physics: equilibrium, diffusion, and waves. We can solve them analytically (separation of variables) or numerically (FDM, FEM, FVM). The deepest insight is that the equation itself tells you the physics. This is what makes PDEs powerful — they're not just symbols, they're a window into how the world works."
