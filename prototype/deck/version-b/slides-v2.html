The Language of Change

Partial Differential Equations

How we describe anything that changes in space AND time

Speaker Notes: Start with the world, not equations. "Every time you check the weather, you're looking at the output of a PDE solver."

From ODE to PDE

ODE — What You Know

One variable changes

$\dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2} = -kx$

A spring oscillating. One dimension. One direction.

PDE — What's New

Multiple variables change

$\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \alpha \dfrac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}$

Temperature on a rod. Position AND time both matter.

Speaker Notes: "A partial derivative is a derivative where you hold some variables fixed. The jump from ODE to PDE is about new geometry."
2

What a PDE Looks Like

A PDE involves an unknown function of several variables and its partial derivatives.
$F\!\left(x,\, y,\, u,\, \frac{\partial u}{\partial x},\, \frac{\partial u}{\partial y},\, \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2},\, \ldots\right) = 0$
$u$
unknown function
$x, y$
independent variables
$\partial$
partial derivative
Speaker Notes: "u is what we're finding. x and y tell us WHERE. The partial derivatives tell us HOW it changes."
3

Three Equations, Three Kinds of Change

Heat Equation

$\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \alpha \nabla^2 u$
Parabolic
A hot spot spreads and smooths. Sharp gradients fade.

Wave Equation

$\dfrac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} = c^2 \nabla^2 u$
Hyperbolic
A pulse travels without fading. Shape preserved.

Laplace Equation

$\nabla^2 u = 0$
Elliptic
No time. Every point equals its neighbors' average.
Speaker Notes: "Heat has ONE time derivative. Wave has TWO. That's why one diffuses and the other propagates."

Classification Determines How You Solve

The type of PDE tells you which solution method to use.
Elliptic → Relaxation methods, potential theory
Parabolic → Separation of variables, Fourier series
Hyperbolic → Characteristics, d'Alembert's formula
The discriminant $B^2 - AC$ determines the type → the type determines the method.
Speaker Notes: "This is the payoff of classification. You don't guess — the equation tells you which method to use."

The Key Trick: Separation of Variables

One hard PDE becomes two easy ODEs.

Step 1: Assume a product

Let $u(x,t) = X(x) \cdot T(t)$. The unknown function is a product of two single-variable functions.

Step 2: Substitute into PDE

The PDE splits into two ODEs: one for $X(x)$, one for $T(t)$. Each is easier to solve.

Step 3: Solve each ODE

$X(x) = \sin(n\pi x/L)$ and $T(t) = e^{-\alpha(n\pi/L)^2 t}$. Standard solutions.

Step 4: Combine

$u(x,t) = \sum B_n \sin(n\pi x/L) \, e^{-\alpha(n\pi/L)^2 t}$. Fourier coefficients from initial condition.

Speaker Notes: "We assume the solution is a product. The PDE splits. Each piece is an ODE we already know how to solve. Then we combine."
