# 06 Conceptual Glossary

## Terms We Must Understand Precisely

This glossary records terms that are容易混淆 (easily confused) or have different meanings in different fields. For each term, we record definition, origin, related concepts, differences, and ambiguities.

---

## presentation

**Definition:** A structured visual and verbal communication event where a speaker presents information to an audience using visual aids (slides, diagrams, etc.).

**Origin:** From Latin *praesentare* (to show, to exhibit). In education and business contexts since the 1990s with the rise of PowerPoint.

**Related concepts:** lecture, talk, speech, demonstration

**Differences:**
- A *presentation* can be formal or informal, educational or persuasive
- A *lecture* implies a teaching context with a teacher-student relationship
- A *talk* is more informal and often implies a shorter duration
- A *speech* focuses on the verbal channel; *presentation* implies multimodal (verbal + visual)

**Ambiguities:** In academic contexts, "presentation" can mean: (1) a conference talk, (2) a lecture, (3) a thesis defense, (4) the slide deck itself. Context usually disambiguates.

---

## lecture

**Definition:** A extended spoken presentation in an educational context, typically delivered by an instructor to students, focused on teaching specific content.

**Origin:** From Latin *lectura* (reading). Originally referred to reading aloud from texts. Evolved to mean extended teaching talk.

**Related concepts:** presentation, seminar, tutorial, recitation

**Differences:**
- A *lecture* is specifically educational; a *presentation* can be any context
- A *lecture* implies a teacher-student relationship; a *presentation* may not
- A *seminar* implies discussion; a *lecture* is primarily one-way
- A *tutorial* implies small group, hands-on instruction

**Ambiguities:** "Lecture" can mean: (1) the act of teaching, (2) a specific session, (3) the content delivered. Also: "giving a lecture" vs. "attending a lecture" have different perspectives.

---

## teaching

**Definition:** The activity of helping someone learn; facilitating the acquisition of knowledge, skills, or understanding.

**Origin:** Old English *tǣcan* (to show, point out, instruct).

**Related concepts:** instruction, education, training, tutoring, facilitation

**Differences:**
- *Teaching* focuses on the process of helping learn
- *Instruction* focuses on the structured delivery of content
- *Education* is broader — includes the whole system of learning
- *Training* focuses on skill acquisition, often for specific tasks
- *Facilitation* implies guiding rather than directing

**Ambiguities:** "Teaching" can mean: (1) the activity of instructing, (2) the profession, (3) the content taught. In our context, we mean the activity — slides should *teach*, not just *inform*.

---

## explanation

**Definition:** The act of making something clear or understandable; the content that clarifies why or how something works.

**Origin:** From Latin *explanare* (to flatten, make clear).

**Related concepts:** description, demonstration, illustration, clarification

**Differences:**
- An *explanation* addresses "why" or "how"
- A *description* addresses "what"
- A *demonstration* shows rather than tells
- An *illustration* provides an example or visual representation

**Ambiguities:** "Explanation" can mean: (1) the act of explaining, (2) the content that explains, (3) a justification. In our context, we mean making complex ideas understandable.

---

## communication

**Definition:** The process of sharing information, ideas, or feelings between entities.

**Origin:** From Latin *communicare* (to share, impart).

**Related concepts:** expression, transmission, dialogue, rhetoric

**Differences:**
- *Communication* implies a two-way process (sender → receiver with feedback)
- *Transmission* implies one-way delivery
- *Expression* focuses on the sender's side
- *Rhetoric* focuses on persuasion

**Ambiguities:** "Communication" can mean: (1) the process, (2) the content communicated, (3) the skill. In academic contexts, there's debate about whether science communication is top-down (deficit model) or participatory.

---

## visualization

**Definition:** The representation of data, information, or ideas in visual form (charts, diagrams, images, animations).

**Origin:** From Latin *visualis* (of or seeing). Formalized in information design by Bertin (1967) and Tufte (1983).

**Related concepts:** graphic, illustration, diagram, chart, image

**Differences:**
- *Visualization* implies data-driven or information-driven visual representation
- *Illustration* can be decorative or explanatory
- *Diagram* implies a schematic representation of relationships
- *Chart* implies quantitative data display
- *Image* is the most general term

**Ambiguities:** "Visualization" can mean: (1) the act of creating visuals, (2) the visual product, (3) the field of study. Also: "scientific visualization" (3D data from simulations) vs. "information visualization" (abstract data) vs. "visual analytics" (interactive exploration).

---

## visual explanation

**Definition:** A visual representation that specifically aims to explain how or why something works, rather than just displaying data.

**Origin:** Tufte (1997) *Visual Explanations: Images and Quantities, Evidence and Narrative*.

**Related concepts:** visualization, diagram, illustration, infographic

**Differences:**
- A *visualization* may just display data
- A *visual explanation* specifically makes a causal or mechanistic claim
- An *infographic* may combine data display with narrative
- A *diagram* may show relationships without explaining mechanism

**Ambiguities:** Not widely used as a formal term outside Tufte's work. In our context, we distinguish between "visual display" (showing data) and "visual explanation" (making ideas understandable).

---

## narrative

**Definition:** A structured sequence of events or ideas organized to create meaning, engagement, and coherence.

**Origin:** From Latin *narrare* (to tell, relate). In communication theory, formalized by narrative transportation theory (Green & Brock, 2000).

**Related concepts:** story, argument, discourse, structure

**Differences:**
- A *narrative* implies temporal or causal sequence
- A *story* implies characters and conflict
- An *argument* implies claims and evidence
- A *discourse* is the broader communicative context

**Ambiguities:** In academic contexts, "narrative" can mean: (1) the story being told, (2) the structure of the presentation, (3) a qualitative research method. We mean (2) — the organizing structure of the presentation.

---

## argument

**Definition:** A set of reasons given with the aim of persuading others that an action or idea is right or wrong.

**Origin:** From Greek *logos* (reason). Formalized in logic and rhetoric.

**Related concepts:** claim, evidence, reasoning, persuasion

**Differences:**
- An *argument* has claims supported by evidence
- A *claim* is an assertion
- *Evidence* supports claims
- *Reasoning* connects evidence to claims

**Ambiguities:** In academic contexts, "argument" can mean: (1) a logical argument, (2) a rhetorical argument, (3) a disagreement. We mean (1) — the logical structure of the presentation's claims.

---

## slide

**Definition:** A single visual display within a presentation, typically one screen or page of content.

**Origin:** From "slide projector" — a physical slide projected onto a screen. Term carried over to digital presentations.

**Related concepts:** frame, page, screen, card, panel

**Differences:**
- A *slide* is the standard term in PowerPoint/Keynote context
- A *frame* is used in animation/video context
- A *page* is used in document context
- A *card* is used in some AI tools (Gamma) and design systems
- A *panel* is used in comic/reveal.js context

**Ambiguities:** "Slide" can mean: (1) the visual display, (2) the content on it, (3) the act of presenting it. In Gamma, "cards" replace slides — this is a meaningful architectural distinction, not just a naming choice.

---

## deck

**Definition:** The complete set of slides in a presentation; the presentation as a whole artifact.

**Origin:** From "deck of cards" — the complete set.

**Related concepts:** slideshow, presentation, deck, deck of slides

**Differences:**
- A *deck* emphasizes the complete artifact
- A *slideshow* emphasizes the sequential display
- A *presentation* can mean the event or the artifact

**Ambiguities:** "Deck" is informal but widely used in business/tech. In academic contexts, "slides" or "presentation" is more common.

---

## learning objective

**Definition:** A statement describing what learners should know, understand, or be able to do after instruction.

**Origin:** Educational psychology; formalized by Bloom (1956), Mager (1962), and others.

**Related concepts:** learning outcome, goal, objective, standard

**Differences:**
- A *learning objective* is specific and measurable
- A *learning outcome* is the actual result
- A *goal* is broader and less specific
- A *standard* is an institutional expectation

**Ambiguities:** "Learning objective" is often used loosely to mean any statement about what should be learned. In instructional design, it specifically means a measurable statement using action verbs from Bloom's taxonomy.

---

## cognitive load

**Definition:** The total amount of mental effort required to process information in working memory during a learning task.

**Origin:** Sweller (1988); formalized in Cognitive Load Theory.

**Related concepts:** mental effort, working memory load, processing demand

**Differences:**
- *Cognitive load* is the technical term from CLT
- *Mental effort* is the subjective experience
- *Working memory load* emphasizes the cognitive architecture
- *Processing demand* emphasizes the task demands

**Ambiguities:** Three types: intrinsic (inherent difficulty), extraneous (caused by poor design), germane (effort toward learning). The germane load concept is debated — some argue it's redundant with intrinsic load.

---

## instructional design

**Definition:** The systematic process of creating learning experiences to make learning more effective, efficient, and engaging.

**Origin:** Military training research (1940s-50s); formalized by Gagné, Merrill, and others.

**Related concepts:** curriculum design, learning design, educational design

**Differences:**
- *Instructional design* focuses on specific learning experiences
- *Curriculum design* focuses on program-level organization
- *Learning design* is a newer, broader term
- *Educational design* is the most general

**Ambiguities:** "Instructional design" can mean: (1) the process, (2) the field, (3) the person who does it (instructional designer). We mean (1) — the systematic process.

---

## slide design

**Definition:** The process of creating individual slides that effectively communicate information and support learning.

**Origin:** Emerged with PowerPoint (1987+); formalized by Duarte (2008), Reynolds (2019), and others.

**Related concepts:** presentation design, visual design, graphic design

**Differences:**
- *Slide design* focuses on individual slides
- *Presentation design* focuses on the whole deck and its structure
- *Visual design* is broader — includes all visual media
- *Graphic design* is the most general visual design discipline

**Ambiguities:** "Slide design" often means "making slides look good." In our context, it means "making slides that teach" — a fundamentally different goal.

---

## presentation design

**Definition:** The holistic process of planning, structuring, and creating a complete presentation, including content, narrative, visuals, and delivery.

**Origin:** Professional practice at firms like Duarte, Inc.; formalized by Duarte (2008) and Reynolds (2019).

**Related concepts:** slide design, information design, communication design

**Differences:**
- *Presentation design* is holistic — includes structure, narrative, and delivery
- *Slide design* is component-level — focuses on individual slides
- *Information design* focuses on clarity and usability of information
- *Communication design* is the broadest term

**Ambiguities:** "Presentation design" often means "making beautiful slides." In our context, it means "designing an effective learning experience" — which includes but goes beyond visual design.

---

## educational technology

**Definition:** The study and ethical practice of facilitating learning and improving performance by creating, using, and managing appropriate technological processes and resources.

**Origin:** Formalized as a field in the 1970s-80s; defined by AECT (Association for Educational Communications and Technology).

**Related concepts:** edtech, e-learning, digital learning, learning technology

**Differences:**
- *Educational technology* is the formal academic field
- *Edtech* is informal shorthand
- *E-learning* focuses on digital delivery
- *Learning technology* is a newer, broader term

**Ambiguities:** Can mean: (1) the field of study, (2) the tools/technologies, (3) the practice. In our context, we mean the application of technology to improve teaching — specifically, using AI to create better academic presentations.
