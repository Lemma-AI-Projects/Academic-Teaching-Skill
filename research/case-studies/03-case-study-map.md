# 03 Case Study Map

## Real-World Academic Presentations: Observations

This map records observations from actual high-quality and notable academic presentations. The purpose is not to extract "best practices" but to accumulate observations before drawing conclusions.

---

## Excellent Cases

### Case 1: Patrick Winston — MIT AI Lectures

- **Source:** MIT OpenCourseWare 6.034 Artificial Intelligence
- **Field:** Computer Science / AI
- **Audience:** Undergraduate students
- **Context:** Semester-long course lectures
- **Duration:** ~50 minutes per lecture
- **Slide count:** Minimal — primarily blackboard and verbal explanation
- **Structure:** Concept introduction → example → formal definition → worked example
- **Visual strategy:** Blackboard drawing, hand-drawn diagrams, minimal text
- **Explanation strategy:** Building from intuition to formalism; cycling through multiple representations
- **Notable characteristics:** Winston deliberately avoided slides for most lectures; used physical props; cycled back to revisit concepts; "empowerment promises" (telling students what they'll be able to do)
- **Possible weaknesses:** Not scalable; requires exceptional presenter skill; no visual persistence for later review
- **Observation:** The most effective lectures used the least slides. This raises the question: are slides sometimes the wrong medium?

### Case 2: Susan McConnell — Stanford Biology

- **Source:** Stanford University biology lectures
- **Field:** Biology / Neuroscience
- **Audience:** Undergraduate and graduate students
- **Context:** Course lectures
- **Duration:** ~50 minutes
- **Slide count:** Moderate
- **Structure:** "Home image" concept — one key image per lecture that anchors all content
- **Visual strategy:** High-quality scientific images; "data dives" where the class analyzes a figure together; transitions between overview and detail
- **Explanation strategy:** Guided analysis of real data; students participate in interpreting figures
- **Notable characteristics:** "Home image" as organizing principle; deliberate use of transitions to show relationships between concepts
- **Possible weaknesses:** Requires extensive preparation of curated images; may not work for all disciplines
- **Observation:** The "home image" concept is powerful — one visual anchor that gives the entire lecture coherence.

### Case 3: Hans Rosling — Gapminder

- **Source:** TED Talks, BBC documentaries
- **Field:** Global Health / Statistics
- **Audience:** General public and policymakers
- **Context:** Conference keynotes and public lectures
- **Duration:** ~20 minutes
- **Slide count:** Moderate (animated charts)
- **Structure:** Challenge assumption → reveal data → narrate story → draw conclusion
- **Visual strategy:** Animated bubble charts (Gapminder software); time-series animations; sportscaster-style narration of data movement
- **Explanation strategy:** "Data narration" — literally commentating on data as it moves, like a sports broadcast
- **Notable characteristics:** Made statistics exciting for general audiences; data became characters in a story; challenged preconceptions systematically
- **Possible weaknesses:** Simplified complex data for public audience; some critics argue he cherry-picked data
- **Observation:** The most effective visualizations were animated, not static. Animation + narration created engagement that static charts could not.

### Case 4: Terence Tao — ICM 2026 Plenary

- **Source:** International Congress of Mathematicians 2026
- **Field:** Mathematics
- **Audience:** Expert mathematicians
- **Context:** Plenary lecture at major conference
- **Duration:** ~60 minutes
- **Slide count:** ~60-80
- **Structure:** Historical context → problem statement → approaches → results → open questions
- **Visual strategy:** Mathematical notation; key theorems highlighted; sparse but precise
- **Explanation strategy:** Goodhart's Law as narrative device; building from simple observations to deep results
- **Notable characteristics:** Combined narrative arc with mathematical rigor; acknowledged collaborative nature of research
- **Possible weaknesses:** Highly specialized — only accessible to expert audience
- **Observation:** Even in the most technical contexts, narrative structure improved clarity.

### Case 5: Demis Hassabis — Nobel Prize Lecture 2024

- **Source:** Nobel Prize in Chemistry, 2024
- **Field:** Computational Biology / AI
- **Audience:** Broad scientific audience (Nobel ceremony)
- **Context:** Prize lecture
- **Duration:** ~45 minutes
- **Slide count:** Moderate
- **Structure:** Hero's journey of science — problem → struggle → breakthrough → implications
- **Visual strategy:** Protein structure visualizations; timeline of discoveries; before/after comparisons
- **Explanation strategy:** Framed protein folding as a grand challenge; used visualizations to show what AI "saw"
- **Notable characteristics:** Made deeply technical content accessible to non-specialists; personal narrative + scientific narrative intertwined
- **Possible weaknesses:** Retrospective narrative may oversimplify the actual research process
- **Observation:** The hero's journey structure worked even for a scientific narrative — but requires careful handling to avoid oversimplification.

### Case 6: David Baker — Nobel Lecture 2024

- **Source:** Nobel Prize in Chemistry, 2024
- **Field:** Computational Biology
- **Audience:** Broad scientific audience
- **Context:** Prize lecture
- **Duration:** ~45 minutes
- **Slide count:** ~80
- **Structure:** List of 5 concrete challenges → narrative through solutions → future directions
- **Visual strategy:** Clear list-based structure; protein design visualizations; team acknowledgments integrated throughout
- **Explanation strategy:** Concrete challenges gave audience a roadmap; each challenge was a self-contained story
- **Possible weaknesses:** The list structure could feel mechanical; required audience to hold multiple threads
- **Observation:** The "5 challenges" structure gave the audience a mental map. Concrete framing > abstract framing.

### Case 7: Doris Tsao — Caltech Lectures

- **Source:** Caltech lectures and conference presentations
- **Field:** Neuroscience
- **Audience:** Mixed (students and researchers)
- **Context:** Course lectures and research talks
- **Duration:** ~50 minutes
- **Slide count:** Moderate to high
- **Structure:** Technical depth + philosophical breadth
- **Visual strategy:** Detailed neuroscience diagrams; clear labeling; gradual reveal of complexity
- **Explanation strategy:** Connected technical details to big-picture questions; made audience care about the "why"
- **Notable characteristics:** Combined technical rigor with intellectual passion; made audience feel the importance of the questions
- **Possible weaknesses:** High information density may overwhelm some audiences
- **Observation:** Passion and intellectual curiosity are visible in slide design — the best slides convey the presenter's engagement with the material.
